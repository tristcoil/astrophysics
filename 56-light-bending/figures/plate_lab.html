Reduce the 1919 plates yourself

On 29 May 1919 two British expeditions photographed the stars beside the eclipsed Sun, at Sobral in Brazil and on the island of Príncipe, to measure how much the Sun's gravity bends starlight: Einstein's theory predicted 1.75″ at the Sun's edge, a Newtonian calculation half as much. The 1920 report by Dyson, Eddington and Davidson printed the micrometer measures of every plate, and whether those measures really decided between the two has been argued over for a century. The measures are public, so you can refit them here: choose the plates, stars and coordinates, or how to treat the plate scale, and the deflection and its uncertainty are recomputed in your browser. A fourth tab is for the 1922 eclipse, observed from Wallal in Western Australia with many more stars.

The plates. Of the eight plates taken with the 4-inch lens during totality, seven show the same seven stars; plate VI, taken through cloud, shows only the prominences. The comparison plates were taken from Sobral on 14–18 July, when the Sun had moved away from the field. At Greenwich each plate was measured against one “scale plate” of the field laid film to film on it, in revolutions of the micrometer screw. The fit. Instead of solving each plate alone, this page fits all the plates you select at once: every plate has its own zero point and linear terms in x and y (scale and rotation), every star has one offset shared by all plates (the scale plate's error at that star, beyond what linear terms can absorb), and a single deflection α multiplies the 1/r pattern on the eclipse plates only. The scatter of each group of measures (eclipse or comparison, R.A. or Dec.) is estimated from its own residuals and sets the weights, and α's error follows from them.

Table view: the 16 plates in 1920 (Table IX) and in your fit

The plates. The astrographic lens, stopped to 8 inches and fed by a 16-inch coelostat, gave 16 measurable plates, but its star images came out blurred; the authors blamed the Sun's heat on the coelostat mirror. The plates were measured in declination only, each against a comparison plate taken in July, and the paper prints the measures of five bright stars (numbers 5, 4, 3, 6 and 10), which are fitted here as Dy = f + d x + e y + α Ey on each plate. Why scale and deflection trade off. Four of the five stars lie north of the Sun and one south of it, so the outward deflection mostly stretches the field in declination, which is what a change of plate scale e also does. From declinations alone the two are only partly separable (see the correlation of α with e above), so every assumption about e moves the deflection.

1920 paper: 1.61″ ± 0.30″ probable error (± 0.44″ at 1σ): the mean of the four comparisons, 1.65″, less 0.04″ for the quarter of Einstein's x shift that was left in when the rotation was found.

The plates. Cloud interfered at Príncipe: of 16 eclipse plates only two, X and W, gave results, with five stars each. They were measured, in units of 0.003″, against plates of the same field taken at Oxford in January (G1 and H1 for X, D1 and I2 for W): both comparisons of X at Príncipe a few days after the eclipse, and W, developed only after the expedition's return, at Cambridge on 22–23 August. Rather than fit the plate scale from the five stars, the 1920 reduction took it from plates of a field near Arcturus photographed both at Oxford and at Príncipe; its uncertainty, 0.14″ probable error or 0.21″ at 1σ at the limb, is common to all four comparisons and is added to the final error unless you let the scale be fitted. The fit. The 1920 reduction used the x measures only to find the rotation between the two plates, after removing three quarters of Einstein's x shift, and the y measures for the deflection. Here each comparison is fitted in both coordinates at once for two zero points, a rotation and the deflection, with the exact GR pattern; the noise is pooled per coordinate over the comparisons you select. The two comparisons of one eclipse plate share that plate's measuring errors, so their mean is taken as √0.75 times as uncertain as one.

Table view: every star

The plates. On 21 September 1922 a Lick Observatory expedition led by W. W. Campbell and R. J. Trumpler photographed the eclipse from Wallal, Western Australia. Two cameras of 15-foot focal length gave 1.72″ ± 0.11″ (1923). Two five-foot cameras with a much wider field recorded many more stars, and the 1928 report lists, for every star, its distance r from the Sun's centre and the radial part of its displacement on each of six plate pairs, with weights (weight 1 is a probable error of 0.33″). The fit. These displacements are relative: each plate's scale was fixed so that the reference stars more than 5° from the Sun average to zero, which takes out their own small deflection and leaves a term that grows in proportion to r. So, as in 1928, each plate pair you select gets its own scale term α, and all their measures are fitted together with one deflection β: D = β (R/r)n − α r, with R = 0.2661° (the radius the 1928 paper adopted) and n = 1 unless you free it; GR predicts β = 1.75″ and n = 1. Each measure is weighted by its printed weight, and the error is scaled up only if the scatter is larger than the weights imply. Leaving the scale terms out shows what they do.

Deflections at 50′ from the Sun's centre are carried to the limb by the 1/r law with the Sun's true radius that day, 15.771′ (the 1920 paper used 15.8′). Errors on this page are 1σ standard errors; the papers of 1920–1928 quoted probable errors (0.6745σ). The page's fits reproduce the project's Python code (eclipse1919.py, principe.py, wallal.py).
