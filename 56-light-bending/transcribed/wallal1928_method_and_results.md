# Wallal 1922, Lick five-foot cameras: method and results

**Source.** W. W. Campbell and R. J. Trumpler, "Observations made with a pair of five-foot cameras on the light-deflections in the Sun's gravitational field at the total solar eclipse of September 21, 1922", *Lick Observatory Bulletin* 13, 130–160 (No. 397), issued March 27, 1928 (ADS `1928LicOB..13..130C`). Printed page *p* is page *p* − 129 of the ADS PDF. All quotes below are verbatim. The original's `1″.82` style is kept inside quotes; elsewhere decimals are written normally.

**Error convention.** Every ± in this paper (and in the 1923 paper) is a **probable error** (p.e. = 0.6745 σ). For example, "the probable error of the final result of our observations is only 5 per cent" (p.151) refers to ±0.09 on 1.75. Multiply by 1.4826 to get a standard error.

**Transcribed tables in this folder:** `wallal1928_table1_plates.csv`, `_table2_refraction_aberration.csv`, `_table3_plate_constants.csv`, `_table4_displacements.csv`, `_table5_checkfield.csv`, `_table6_radial.csv`, `_table7_groups.csv`, `_table7_allplates.csv`, `_table8_checkfield_groups.csv`, `_table9_limb.csv`, `_table10_combined.csv`, `_table11_groups.csv`, `_table12_best15.csv`, `wallal1928_areas.csv`, `wallal1928_results.csv` (every printed solution) and `wallal1923_table2_displacements.csv`. Checks and discrepancies are in `wallal1928_notes.md`.

## 1. Instruments (p.130)

The Crocker Eclipse Expedition used two pairs of cameras at Wallal, Western Australia:

- a twin camera of **15 feet** focal length with 5-inch doublet objectives (four plates; results published in 1923, LOB 11, 41), and
- a twin camera of **5 feet** focal length with "two wide-angle quadruplet objectives of four inches aperture". In the scan a pen stroke crosses out the words "designed by Dr. F. E. Ross" that follow.

> "The two pairs of instruments used plates of the same size (17 x 17 inches). The 15-foot cameras thus served to photograph the immediate surroundings of the Sun (5° x 5°) on a large scale (45″ to the mm) and were best suited for observing the stars nearest to the Sun's limb. The 5-foot cameras, on the other hand, covered a large field (15° x 15°) on a smaller scale (135″ to the mm) and were planned principally to determine the law according to which the light deflections diminish with increasing angular distance from the Sun's center." (p.130)

The two objectives of the twin camera are called **M** and **N**. The two images on one plate pair were exposed at the same moment. The guiding telescope had a 6-inch objective of 6½ feet focal length (p.130). A footnote (p.130, signed by Campbell) says that Campbell organized the expedition and took the Wallal exposures. Trumpler took the Tahiti comparison plates. The measurements, "the elaborate computations based upon these measurements, and the extensive discussion of the final and general results were made at Mount Hamilton in the past two years exclusively by him [Trumpler]".

## 2. Plates (pp.130–132, Table 1)

- **Comparison plates.** These were taken at night at **Tahiti, May 24–30, 1922**, "four months before the eclipse" (pp.130–131). The eclipse field was exposed between 9h and 9h30m in the evening. The plates were then left in the telescope until 3h15m–3h45m in the morning and exposed to the check field. M7 and N9 were the exception: the check field came first and the eclipse field followed the next evening (p.131). Exposures were 3 minutes on both fields. Plate glass "17 x 17 inches in size, ¼ inch thick, coated with Seed 23 emulsion" (p.131).
- **Check field.** It was centred at "R. A. 18h5m.5, Decl. +1° 1′ (following the eclipse star field by 6h14m.9)":
  > "At the date of the eclipse as well as at the time of the Tahiti photographs this check field was more than 90° distant from the Sun and therefore unaffected by any light deflections; in both cases moreover it could be observed at night. This check field was to be photographed on the same plates as the eclipse star field, at the same hour-angle, during the same day, and no alterations were to be made at the telescope between the two exposures except opening and closing the plateholder slides." (pp.130–131)
- **Wallal exposures (p.131).** A 1-minute check-field exposure was taken on Sept. 20 at 7h45m p.m. Then, during totality on Sept. 21:
  - a 1-minute exposure "started 10 seconds after the beginning of totality";
  - a second pair of plates, exposed for 102 seconds;
  - a third exposure of 62 seconds, "completed a few seconds before the end of totality". The last pair got a 1-minute check-field exposure at 7h45m that evening.

  > "Six eclipse plates were thus available; four of these had an exposure of about 1 minute on the eclipsed Sun and an exposure of equal duration on the check field; two plates had an eclipse exposure of 1m42s, but no check field exposure." (p.131)
- **Pairs measured (Table 1, p.132).** Each pair is eclipse plate − Tahiti plate: M15–M7, M16–M11, M17–M9, N18–N11, N19–N13, N20–N9. M16 and N19 (102 s) have no check-field exposure. Table 1 gives the dates, local mean times, exposures, hour angles, temperatures and barometer readings (`wallal1928_table1_plates.csv`). The times are in astronomical reckoning, counted from noon. The Wallal eclipse exposures at "1h30.7m" etc. on Sept. 21 are 1:30–1:35 p.m. local mean time, about 5h28m–5h32m UT (Wallal 8h2m.7 E). The Tahiti check-field times "15h…" are early-morning exposures.
- **Image quality (pp.131–132).**
  > "On the 5-foot camera plates the corona is very strong and extends as a general, nearly circular halo to about 2° from the Sun's center." (p.131)

  The N images are "uniformly good all over the field". The M images are "slightly distorted" in the S.E. corner, but "the distortion is the same as on the Tahiti comparison plates". Those M images were still used, with smaller weight (p.132).

## 3. Star list and weights (pp.132–133)

The program had 147 stars (p.132):

1. all measurable images within 3° of the Sun's centre (55 stars);
2. about 16 stars, four per quadrant, in each 1°-wide ring between 3° and 7° (66 stars);
3. 6–7 stars in each corner of the plate (26 stars).

> "Two stars (66 and 90) of the first group were later dropped because they were found too faint for measurement. The number was thus reduced to 145." (p.132)

Table 6 gives the matching counts: 53 stars inside 3°, 66 between 3° and 7°, and 26 beyond 7°.

Table 4 lists, for each star:

- the B.D. number or name;
- α and δ for 1900;
- the photographic magnitude (mostly HDC);
- the "combined proper-motion and parallax corrections to be applied to the differential measures Eclipse-Night" ("they are small, and exceed 0″.1 only in four cases"; the four are stars 55, 58, 69 and 115);
- rectangular coordinates x, y, "referred to the Sun's center at the middle of the eclipse as zero point, … expressed in units of 200 mm or 27084″, and oriented to the pole of 1922.0" (p.132).

The check field has 75 stars, "mostly of magnitudes 7–8" (p.132).

Each measure was weighted by the quality of its eclipse-plate image (p.133):

| Quality of image | Weight |
|---|---|
| Good | 1. |
| Fair | 0.7 |
| Poor, faint | 0.4 |
| Very poor, very faint | 0.25 |

> "The weights were fixed quite mechanically by four independent estimates of the image quality, one at every measurement, the mean of the four corresponding weights being adopted." (p.133)

## 4. Measurement (p.133)

Each eclipse plate was compared directly with one Tahiti plate, using "a new method described by Trumpler" (*PASP* 36, 9, 1924). The two plates were laid film to film with a small offset. The lower plate was brought into focus through "an optically plane parallel glass plate, 10.6 mm thick". The plates were oriented by four stars near the edge. Each coordinate was measured "direct" and "reversed" (the plate turned through 180°). The plate was worked in 8 zones, and the important stars near the Sun were measured four times. In all, "about 37,000 bisections on 2,280 star images" were made (p.133).

Micrometer screw values were found from six wide double stars (p.133):

| Pair | M15–M7 | M16–M11 | M17–M9 | N18–N11 | N19–N13 | N20–N9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| screw value | 23″.85 | 22.93 | 23.85 | 23.80 | 23.84 | 20.40 |

## 5. Reduction (pp.133–137)

The measured differences Δx, Δy combine ten effects (pp.133–134):

1. offset;
2. orientation;
3. scale;
4. plate inclination;
5. optical distortion;
6. differential refraction;
7. differential aberration;
8. proper motion and parallax, which "were neglected for the check field stars, but were applied to the stars of the eclipse field";
9. light deflection;
10. errors of observation.

Refraction and aberration were computed to third order (Table 2). After a trial with first- and second-order terms only on the N20–N9 check field, "another solution including also third order terms led to considerably smaller and better distributed residuals" (p.134). The reduction formula is therefore the complete cubic, with 20 constants (p.135):

> Δx + a + bx + cy + dx² + exy + fy² + gx³ + hx²y + ixy² + ky³ = D_x + v_x (and the same for Δy with primed coefficients)
>
> "Δx and Δy are the differential measures taken in the sense Eclipse-Night, and corrected in case of the eclipse field for proper-motion and parallax. D_x and D_y are the components of the star displacements due to light deflection; they vanish, of course, for the check-field." (p.135)

**Second- and third-order terms came from the check field.**

> "The important point of our reduction method is, that the coefficients of the second and third order terms, which are due only to the inclination of the plate (4) and to optical distortions (5), when corrected for refraction, aberration, etc., must be the same for the eclipse field as for the check field photographed on the same plate. They can then be determined from the latter without any assumption about the light-deflections." (p.135)

The 20 check-field constants of the four pairs with check exposures were found from the 75 check stars. The method splits the plate into zones parallel to each axis; it is "shorter and nearly as good" as a strict least-squares solution (pp.135–136). Table 3 lists the constants as determined ("Check F. det."). The "Check F. corr." column has refraction, aberration and a small inclination term deducted; that term arises because the check-field centre differs slightly from the eclipse-field centre (Table 2). Adding the eclipse-field refraction and aberration gives "Eclipse F. adopt." (p.136).

**Linear terms and the scale.** These came from the eclipse field itself, using the outer stars, with their deflections neglected:

> "After correcting the eclipse field measures for second and third order terms, the coefficients of the linear terms a, b, c; a′, b′, c′ were determined by least squares solution from the residuals of 55 reference stars which are more than 5° distant from the Sun's center. In this solution the light deflections of the reference stars, which must be small, were neglected. This does not affect the orientation constants, but leads to an erroneous scale value. The scale of the eclipse field photographs is necessarily linked together with the light deflections; the two cannot be separated unless some assumption is made concerning the law according to which the light deflections are related to the star's angular distance from the Sun's center. Our reduction method is therefore equivalent to applying an arbitrary scale value so chosen that the residuals become zero for the reference stars on the average, i. e., at a mean distance of 7°.3 from the Sun's center. Our observations do not furnish the absolute values of the light deflection, but the relative displacement of each star as compared with the mean of the reference stars situated in the outer portions of the field." (pp.136–137)

The scale was therefore **not** taken from the check field and **not** assumed. It was set from the reference stars beyond 5°, which leaves every displacement relative to them. The remaining scale term α was later **solved for together with the deflection** in the final fit (§8). The authors say explicitly (p.138):

> "It had never been the intention of the observers to use the check field photographs for determining the scale of the eclipse photographs. Although both were made on the same plate it was anticipated that the scale of the eclipse exposure would differ from that of the check field photographed at night; for the scale is quite sensitive to temperature changes affecting the camera tubes as well as the photographic plates."

**Zero points** (p.137):

> "The final values of the zero corrections a, a′ were not taken from the least squares solutions of the reference stars, but determined from all stars of the eclipse field (except those within 1°.5 of the Sun's center which have rather large displacements), giving equal weight to the area between 1°.5 and 4°.5 from the Sun's center and to that outside of this limit and dividing the weight equally among the four quadrants."

**M16 and N19** had no check field. "The unknown effects of light deflection were first eliminated by forming the means of the observed star displacements of the other four plates and deducting these from the differential measures of the two plates M16 and N19." The 20 constants were then found from 8 y-zones, excluding stars within 1° of the Sun (p.137).

**Output** (p.137):

> "After correcting the differential measures Eclipse-Night for all known sources of error according to the formula of p. 135 (with the coefficients 'Check F. det.' and 'Eclipse F. adopt.' of Table 3) we obtain the data of Tables 4 and 5. For the eclipse field they represent the combined effect of apparent star displacements due to light deflection, error in the adopted scale of the plates, and errors of observation; for the check field they should have the character of accidental measuring errors."

**Rejections.** "All measures rejected on account of large residuals are entered in Tables 4 and 5 in brackets" (p.137); these are the parentheses in the print. Film deformations near the southern plate edge led to rejecting "all star images of the eclipse field as well as of the check field … within the affected area". There are 17 rejected eclipse-field measures (11 stars) and 7 rejected check-field residuals.

## 7. Plate constants and the scale difference (pp.138, 145–146)

The scale corrections b and c′ (corrected for refraction and aberration) of check field and eclipse field differ systematically:

> "Indeed we find between the differential scale corrections of the eclipse and check fields a marked systematic difference (column 'check-eclipse') which on the average amounts to 1″.15 per unit of 7°.52, or 0″.153 per 1°." (p.138)

The paper accounts for this difference per degree (p.145):

| | 5-foot | 15-foot |
|---|---|---|
| Scale diff. between ecl. and check field | 0″.153 | 0″.415 |
| Light deflection of ref. stars (Einstein) | −.009 | −.064 |
| Temperature expansion | −.040 | −.040 |
| Unaccounted for | 0″.104 | 0″.311 |

The remainder is attributed to the telescope tube being heated relative to the plate ("a difference of 2°.5C … is sufficient", p.145). Using the check-field scale instead "would furnish quite incompatible results for the light deflections of the stars situated between 2° and 3° from the Sun's center (5-foot cameras :0″.48, 15-foot cameras :0″.94)" (p.145).

The third-order terms matter for the 1/r law. They "may seriously affect the law according to which the observed light deflections depend on the star's angular distance … without the check field exposures it would hardly have been possible to separate the effects of these third order correction terms from the light deflections. By chance it happened that the means of the four important constants: g = +″.22, k′ = −″.10; i = −″.47, h′ = +″.06 are small, so that our final results were little influenced by them." (pp.145–146)

## 8. Results of the five-foot cameras (pp.146–151)

**Eight areas and the magnitude equation** (pp.146–147, `wallal1928_areas.csv`). The weighted mean displacements of 8 areas show displacements away from the Sun in the four central areas. The outer and central means differ slightly. The authors tested a magnitude equation ("bright-faint Δx = −″.08 Δy = −″.02") and corrections of the second-order terms (Δd = +″.03, Δf = +.06, Δd′ = +″.10, Δf′ = +.20, Δa′ = −″.07). They concluded:

> "the systematic difference between the four central and the four outer areas can be mainly attributed to the accidental errors of the second order plate constants. With the symmetrical distribution of our stars, however, these terms have practically no influence on the light deflections derived in the mean from all stars." (p.147)

**Radial components** (p.147):

> "Our observations having confirmed the assumption that the light deflections operate radially away from the Sun's center, the radial components D_r of the observed star displacements D_x and D_y were computed according to the formula: D_r = D_x x/r + D_y y/r (r = √(x² + y²)). They are given for the eclipse field in Table 6 in which the stars are arranged according to their angular distances from the Sun's center."

- Each D_r carries the weight of its plate measure (Table 4 weights).
- The "Mean" column of Table 6 is the weighted mean, and its weight is the sum of the plate weights. The transcription reproduces both for all 145 stars.
- Table 7 forms group means in 9 distance groups for each plate pair and each objective. The radial displacement "stands out plainly for every single plate" (p.147).
- The check-field equivalent (Table 8) shows "no evidence of systematic displacements … four of the seven values are smaller, three larger than the probable error" (p.150; group p.e. ±0″.04).

**Comparison with Einstein, group means** (p.150). Absolute theory is converted to relative displacements "by a scale correction of −″.009r, where r is the star's angular distance from the Sun's center in degrees":

> "The agreement between observation and theory is quite as good as can be expected from the accidental measuring errors. The only larger differences are found among the first three groups, which contain fewer stars and have less weight. More than half of the residuals: 'Obs.-Theory' are smaller than the probable error of the corresponding group mean." (p.150)

**Deflection at the limb, Table 9** (pp.150–151):

> "Assuming with Einstein that the light deflections are inversely proportional to the stars' angular distances from the Sun's center, we can determine for every plate the light deflection at the Sun's limb (β) and the additional scale correction (α) to be applied to our measures. The two unknowns α and β were obtained from the data of Table 6 by least squares solutions with the following equations of condition: −αr + β R/r = D_r; R = radius of Sun's disk = 0°.2661." (p.150)

The deflection β and the residual scale α are therefore solved **simultaneously**, star by star, for each of the six plate pairs. Refitting the transcribed Table 6 reproduces the printed weights of β to within 0.007 and α to within 0.0012. β agrees to within 0.074, much less than its p.e. of about ±0″.39.

The ±0″.15 equals 0″.33/√5.14, where 5.14 is the sum of the six weights. The weight unit is the adopted p.e. of unit weight, ±0″.33.

> "The constant β is extrapolated a long way from relatively small observed star displacements and has merely the significance of the numerical coefficient in an interpolation formula. For this reason the individual values of β have small weight and show a considerable range. If their differences from the mean, 1″.82, are used for computing the probable error of unit weight we obtain ±″.37, which is very little in excess of the value ±″.33 derived on page 138 from the mean displacements of each star. … The mean result 1″.82 agrees quite closely with the value 1″.75 predicted by Einstein's Theory." (p.151)

The paper gives the 5-foot result as **1″.82 ± 0″.15 (probable error)**: Table 9 (p.151) and summary item 3 (p.160).

## 9. Combination with the 15-foot result (pp.151–153)

> "The results of the 5-foot cameras confirm in every way those of the 15-foot cameras and this justifies combining the data of the two instruments. For the light deflection at the Sun's limb we have:" (p.151)

| | Defl. at Sun's limb | |
|---|---|---|
| 15-foot cameras | 1″.72 ± ″.11 | (weight 2) |
| 5-foot cameras | 1.82 ± .15 | (weight 1) |
| **Mean** | **1″.75 ± .09** | (weight 3) |
| Einstein's Theory | 1.75 | |

> "The mean taken with weights adopted according to the probable errors coincides exactly with the prediction of Einstein's Generalized Theory of Relativity, and the probable error of the final result of our observations is only 5 per cent." (p.151)

The paper lists six ways the two series differ (p.151): objectives; focal length, scale and field; emulsion (Seed 23 vs Seed 30); measuring method; reduction (third-order terms used only for the 5-foot plates); and the stars used. The 5-foot result rests on stars between 1° and 3° compared with reference stars at 5°–10°. The 15-foot result rests on stars between 0°.5 and 1°.5 compared with reference stars at 2°–3°.5.

**Star-by-star combination** (Tables 10 and 11, pp.152–153):

> "For the differential measure (radial component) of a pair of good star images the two instruments furnished the following probable errors: 15-foot cameras ±″.20 (±.0044 mm), 5-foot cameras ±.34 (±.0025 mm) … According to these figures we have to divide the weights of the 5-foot camera measures by 3 to make them comparable with the weight system of the 15-foot cameras." (p.152)

> "The star displacements observed with the 15-foot cameras are relative to a set of reference stars situated between 2° and 3°.5 from the Sun's center. Most of these (26) were also observed with the 5-foot cameras which give for them an average radial displacement of +0″.15 while their average distance from the Sun's center is 2°.52. A scale correction of +″.060 per 1° is, therefore, needed to reduce the results of the 15-foot cameras to the system of the 5-foot cameras" (p.152)

The 15-foot values used are the directly observed D₁ of the 1923 Table II, not the check-star-corrected D₂ (p.152). Table 10 lists all stars within 3°.5. Table 11 gives 11 group means: groups 1–7 come from Table 10, and groups 8–11 are the Table 7 all-plate groups with their weights divided by 3.

## 10. Test of the 1/r law and comparison with Einstein (pp.153–155)

The paper fits **no free exponent**. The law is tested by comparing the group means of Tables 7 and 11 with Einstein's 1/r curve (Fig. 3) and with Courvoisier's "yearly refraction" (Fig. 5). Both curves first have a scale correction found by least squares.

> "For Einstein's Generalized Theory of Relativity the scale correction was found to be +″.009r and this was deducted from the data of Einstein's formula: 1″.75 R/r, R = radius of Sun's disk = 0°.2661, r = star's angular distance from Sun's center" (p.154)

> "The residuals between observation and theory are in distribution and size (6 being smaller, 5 larger than the probable errors) such as would be expected from the theory of errors. The four successive residuals between 1° and 2°.5 can hardly claim any real significance, as even their mean amounts only to +″.035, and any arbitrary smooth interpolation curve drawn close to the plotted group means will not differ from the theoretical curve by more than 0″.03–0″.04." (p.154)

Table 12 lists the x and y displacements of the 15 best-measured stars within 2°.5 (weight ≥ 4.6). "The agreement between observation and theory is surprisingly close" (p.155). The conclusion:

> "The combined observations of the two instruments thus confirm that the light deflections are directed radially away from the Sun's center, while their amount is inversely proportional to the angular distance from the Sun's center, and they give for the numerical coefficient (deflection at the Sun's limb) the exact value 1″.75 predicted by Einstein's Theory." (p.155)

**Courvoisier** (pp.155–157). Courvoisier's formula is 0″.55 (1 − √(sin r/2)). After "a scale correction of ″.073 per 1°, determined by a least squares solution to reduce the group residuals to a minimum" (p.155; the Table 11 column heading on p.153 prints it as "−″.0735r"):

> "The representation of our observations by the Courvoisier formula is quite unsatisfactory. Two of the group means differ from the curve by inadmissibly large amounts (5–6 times the probable error), and all seven group means between 1°.5 and 6° have large negative residuals." (p.155)

## 11. Terrestrial refraction (pp.157–160)

- The authors model the tilt of equal-density layers in the Moon's shadow (Table 13, a model atmosphere; not transcribed). The anomalous refraction comes out at ±0″.001 for stars at 1° and 7°.5 (p.159).
- They redo Varnum's "prismatic refraction" estimate. For a star directly above or below the Sun it gives 0″.22, not his 1″.82. This effect "would apply to our reference stars in nearly the same amount as to the stars close to the Sun" and "could thus in no way explain the star displacements observed" (p.159).

## 12. Summary as printed (p.160)

> "3. On every plate the stars near the eclipsed Sun appear displaced away from the Sun's center as compared with the reference stars situated in the outer portions of the plate, and the observations of this instrument lead to a value of 1″.82 ± ″.15 for the light deflection at the Sun's limb. The check field stars show no such displacements."
>
> "5. Combining the measures of the two instruments, the relative displacements of stars between 0°.5 and 10° from the Sun's center are established with a high degree of accuracy (Table 11) independently of any theoretical assumptions."
>
> "6. Within the limits set by the unavoidable errors of observation the star displacements are directed radially away from the Sun's center."
>
> "7. Einstein's law that the light deflections are inversely proportional to the angular distance from the Sun's center represents the observations best. With this law the combined observations of the two instruments furnish a value of 1″.75 ± ″.09 for the deflection at the Sun's limb which agrees exactly with Einstein's prediction."
>
> "8. The eclipse observations contradict the existence of Courvoisier's 'yearly refraction' at least in the immediate neighborhood of the Sun."
>
> "9. Abnormal refraction in the Earth's atmosphere caused by the cooling effects of the Moon's shadow could not have affected these measures appreciably."
