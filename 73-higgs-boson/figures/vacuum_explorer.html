Is our vacuum stable?

The Higgs field's self-coupling λ depends on the energy at which you probe it. The measured Higgs and top-quark masses fix λ near 173 GeV; the renormalization-group equations carry it upward. The heavy top quark drags it down. If λ turns negative below the Planck mass, a lower-energy state of the Higgs field exists and the empty space we live in is only metastable. How it is computed

Couplings at μ = Mt come from the measured masses (a fit to this project's matching, calibrated to SMDR by Martin & Robertson). They then run with the Standard Model's three-loop β functions (Buttazzo et al. 2013, appendix B) up to 1020 GeV, recomputed in your browser at every move.

What the verdicts mean

Stable: λ stays positive up to the Planck mass, 1.22 × 1019 GeV. The stricter test with the two-loop effective potential (dashed blue line) moves that border by about 0.07 GeV. Unstable: a bubble of the true vacuum would probably have formed in our past light cone (leading-order tunnelling rate, Planck 2018 cosmology). Metastable: in between.

What it assumes

No new particles or forces up to the Planck scale, which is a big assumption, and the top mass measured at colliders taken as the pole mass (an ambiguity of a few hundred MeV). Tunnelling rates are leading-order estimates; Andreassen, Frost & Schwartz (2018) give the full next-to-leading-order calculation.
